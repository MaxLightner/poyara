---
title: "Before Selling a Tool, Learn the Work"
summary: "The first useful customer conversation often begins before a pitch, with an effort to understand the work as it actually happens."
date: 2026-10-22
topic: "Market discovery"
---

A product builder knows the proposed solution unusually well. That familiarity can become a problem during the first conversation with a potential customer.

The builder wants to explain the interface, demonstrate the features and prove how much thought went into the system. The other person may be thinking about a completely different problem—or may not be looking to buy anything that day.

## An introduction is not yet a discovery conversation

Finding a business owner or obtaining contact information is rarely the most important intellectual challenge. Learning how the business actually works is harder.

People who use a process every day can reveal details that a decision-maker never mentions: interruptions, informal workarounds, information that is copied twice and responsibilities that become unclear during busy periods. None of this is guaranteed to appear in an initial product pitch.

Before asking someone to consider a solution, it can be more useful to ask:

- What step regularly slows the work down?
- What happens when the normal process fails?
- Who notices the problem first, and who must resolve it?
- Which existing workaround would be difficult to give up?

These questions are not a script for extracting approval. They are ways of checking whether our model of the work is accurate.

## Non-sales conversations can be more informative

Someone who is not being asked to purchase may have less reason to defend the current system or reject a proposal on the spot. Such conversations can also lead to a more relevant introduction later.

But informal feedback is not demand. Hearing that an idea sounds helpful does not establish willingness to adopt, pay for or maintain it. Those questions require separate tests.

## Move from understanding to evidence

The useful progression is not always pitch → demo → sale. Sometimes it is observation → clearer problem → narrower demo → trial under real conditions.

For POYARA, that is an important discipline: avoid treating access to a market as proof that the product fits it. **First understand the work. Then show how the experience changes it.**
